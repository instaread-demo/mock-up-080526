Doug Ford is wrong: Why doing nothing at all is Canada’s best response to Trump’s tariffs

Ontario Premier Doug Ford empties a Crown Royal bottle of whisky in Kitchener, Ont., Sept. 2, 2025. Sammy Kogan/The Canadian Press.

Trevor Tombe
30 Jul 26

Canada’s best response to the latest round of U.S. tariffs may be to do nothing at all.

Not literally nothing. Governments will naturally help workers and communities facing serious disruption. And we should make our economy more productive, competitive, and resilient.

But when it comes to retaliation, doing nothing may be the wisest course.

That will strike many Canadians as wrong. Early in the trade war, roughly three in four said Ottawa should retaliate with tariffs of its own. A similar share supported restricting exports of electricity, wood, and oil.

Premiers have echoed that. Ontario’s Doug Ford has been the most forceful last week, calling on Ottawa to go after energy, potash, minerals, and electricity. They “need to feel the pain rather than us constantly feeling the pain,” he said last week, adding quite strongly that Canada could “dismantle the U.S. if we wanted to.” He framed retaliation as not merely an option, but an obligation: “I feel some days that I’m standing alone protecting Canada and I need everyone else to get onside.”

The prime minister, for his part, has been more cautious, but still says “everything is on the table.”

So what should Canada do?

Retaliation has one job

A useful answer comes from Adam Smith, whom my colleague Professor Kent Fellows has regularly (and wisely) cited on this point.

Writing in The Wealth of Nations 250 years ago, Smith argued that retaliation may be justified “when there is a probability that they will procure the repeal of the high duties or prohibitions complained of.”

When there is no such probability, “it seems a bad method of compensating the injury done to certain classes of our people, to do another injury ourselves.”

That is the test we should all have in mind.

A rally beside the Ambassador Bridge in Windsor, Ont., on Saturday, April 26, 2025. Sean Kilpatrick/The Canadian Press.

The purpose of retaliation should be to change the other country’s behaviour. It should not be to express anger, satisfy a desire for revenge, or create the appearance of action.

I have no particular expertise on the president’s motivations, and I am happy to defer to those who do (although really, does anybody?). But the record is not encouraging. And there is little reason to think Canadian retaliation would change U.S. policy. Canada is far too small for retaliatory tariffs to do much damage to the U.S. economy.

The tariffs we imposed last year were, in practice, taxes on Canadian consumers and Canadian businesses. And even if we were large enough, it seems a stretch to suppose that a president who prizes “winning” over all else would respond by backing down.

Perhaps this isn’t actually a trade war

There’s another way to think about all of this. One that doesn’t consider it a trade war at all.

The United States is a sovereign country. It can adopt whatever policies it likes, including ones that damage its own economy. Tariffs are taxes on Americans, not on Canadians.

It feels like we are being targeted. Mostly, we are not. The data make that clear. Consider the distribution of tariffs that the United States now applies across a wide variety of products and countries. Below, I plot roughly 51,000 tariff lines in total (for the trade geeks, this is all country-HS4 combinations).

Graphic Credit: Janice Nelson.

Plenty of individual Canadian products face high rates. Many steel and aluminum items already face 50 percent rates, for example, and many more will on August 19. But on the whole, Canada is not singled out. If anything, it’s the reverse.

The same holds at the country level, where U.S. tariffs are landing on nearly every country on earth.

Graphic Credit: Janice Nelson.

This is a fundamental change in American economic policy, not a policy aimed at us.

Of course, within Canada, the effects are uneven.

Once the 50 percent tariffs take effect on August 19, some provinces will face a considerable drag. But even those provinces do not stand out by international standards. My own calculations on this are illustrated below, with Quebec topping the chart at over 13 percent.

Graphic Credit: Janice Nelson.

This does not make the tariffs harmless, but it does put them in perspective.

Bad American tax policy

A better way to think about all this is as bad American tax policy. Suppose Washington doubled its corporate income tax, or raised top marginal rates on personal income to 80 or 90 percent. Growth south of the border would slow. Canadian firms would feel it. The United States is both a major buyer of what we produce and a major supplier of the inputs we use.

Bad American tax policy would hurt us.

But it would not cross anyone’s mind to retaliate. We would recognize it as an unfortunate choice the Americans made, and then get on with our own business.

Tariffs (at least as we’re seeing them now) deserve the same treatment. Our best response is the same one we would choose if the United States had adopted sensible policies instead: focus on what is under our control.

Anger is not a strategy

The rhetorical attacks coming from the president make that difficult. They have increased Canadian anger toward his administration and its policies. But while threats against our very sovereignty, or incoherent threats of tariffing wildfire smoke, may make it feel like we’re a unique target, the actual tariff schedules say something else.

There is also a great deal of incoherence in U.S. policymaking. Announcements can be abrupt, agreements temporary, and policies regularly change with little warning. Canada cannot control that uncertainty. But we can control much of what happens here.

From recognizing professional credentials across the country, to improving competition, to making it easier to build infrastructure and major projects, to reforming taxes that discourage investment. We can also improve Canada’s ability to sell goods and services to markets beyond the United States by (as the EU has) reaching agreements and deals with major economies from Argentina and Brazil to India.

We can also lower artificial costs on Canadian consumers. There is surprisingly little public pressure, for example, to improve and free markets for dairy, poultry, and other products.

The more energy we spend debating how to elbow the United States, the less attention we give to the policy choices that are actually ours to make.

Perhaps another analogy may also be helpful; think of U.S. policy like we do a hurricane, and respond similarly. We support the people harmed. We help them rebuild. We invest in resilience so the next one does less damage. We don’t shake our fists at the sky and demand the storms stop coming. We certainly don’t “retaliate” by imposing taxes on ourselves.

Do nothing, then. Not nothing at home, where there is a great deal to do. But nothing aimed at Washington.

Whether the United States finds its footing again soon does not depend much on us.

Whether Canada does is entirely up to us.

Trump’s tariffs are not an attack on Canada’s economy. They’re an attack on America’s. We should respond accordingly.

The 2026 World Cup may turn a rare profit—but will the host countries? 

Analysis 30 July 2026

Prime Minister Mark Carney and FIFA President Gianni Infantino hold the World Cup trophy in Ottawa, May 24, 2026. Justin Tang/The Canadian Press.

Prime Minister Mark Carney and FIFA President Gianni Infantino hold the World Cup trophy in Ottawa, May 24, 2026. Justin Tang/The Canadian Press.

Alicia Planincic
30 Jul 26

The dust is settling after Spain’s victory over Argentina in the World Cup final, bringing a close to the five-week tournament jointly hosted by Canada, the U.S., and Mexico and watched around the world.

With the trophy having been awarded, our minds are shifting from the pitch to the balance sheet, raising the question: did the tournament make money and, perhaps more importantly, was it a good deal for the host countries?

These sorts of events tend to rake in loads of money, but they also come with exorbitant costs, and, historically, costs have exceeded revenues. In fact, almost every World Cup since the mid-1960s has failed to cover its costs. Sports economics research comparing direct revenues (ticket sales, broadcasting, and sponsorship) and costs (venue costs and tournament organization) finds that the World Cup has persistently run a deficit, despite substantial government support.

The same is true of major sporting events and tournaments more broadly. Since the mid-1960s, only three Summer Olympics (1984 Los Angeles, 1996 Atlanta, 2000 Sydney), two Winter Olympics (1984 Sarajevo, 2010 Vancouver), and two World Cups (2006 Germany, 2018 Russia) brought in more money than they cost.

But there’s reason to think the 2026 World Cup may have bucked that trend, with the tournament on track to be one of the most profitable World Cups, and perhaps the most profitable, major sports tournament on record. According to BCA’s estimates, profits could total as much as $3 billion, based on official revenue projections and estimated costs.

A major reason for this is that all 16 stadiums used for the tournament already existed. While some upgrades were needed, such as Toronto’s, with a price tag of $158 million, none were newly constructed, dramatically reducing one of the largest costs of hosting. By contrast, some of the most expensive World Cups involved major stadium construction efforts: 2002 Japan and South Korea required 16 new stadiums, while 2022 Qatar required seven.

Graphic credit: Janice Nelson

While the 2026 World Cup may be in the black—the organizer, FIFA, almost certainly made money—not every party involved will necessarily come out ahead. In fact, the profits generated directly by the tournament are largely enjoyed by FIFA, not the hosts.

Meanwhile, the three host countries, which foot a large portion of the bill, may still see a net loss. For instance, even with existing infrastructure, Canada spent over $1 billion across various levels of government, amounting to about $82 million per game for the 13 games held in Canada.

Of course, there is an upside: local industries benefit from increased spending on hotels, restaurants, and bars—but not as much as you might expect. Research suggests these gains largely reflect a shift in consumer spending from other activities and tend to be short-lived, limiting the net boost to the economy or government revenues.

A Germany fan takes his seat at Toronto Stadium for Germany’s World Cup group stage match against Ivory Coast in Toronto on Saturday June 20, 2026. Chris Young/The Canadian Press.

Yet, for decades, countries have enthusiastically competed for the right to host. This suggests the value proposition may go beyond the balance sheet to benefits that are either intangible or harder to quantify. Hosting a major tournament might put a country on the map for tourists, showcase it to investors, or build national pride. And, to a lesser extent, there could be some longer-term returns on investment. For example, while Calgary’s 1988 Winter Olympics didn’t cover its direct costs, it led to infrastructure developments used in the decades since, such as the University’s Olympic Oval, the LRT extension, and upgrades to Canada Olympic Park.

The economics may never cleanly settle the debate on whether hosting “the beautiful game” is financially beneficial. But if the 2026 World Cup is as profitable as projections indicate, it suggests locations with existing infrastructure have a major advantage—something to keep in mind the next time Canada or a Canadian city considers bidding to host. The next World Cup, jointly hosted by Spain, Portugal, and Morocco, three countries with varying levels of existing infrastructure, will further put this to the test.

No matter what the cost, there’s no doubt the world will be watching.

Trump vows tariffs on Canada over wildfire smoke drifting into U.S. cities

Analysis 29 July 2026

The U.S. Capitol is seen, as smoke from wildfires blankets the sky, Friday, July 17, 2026, in Washington. Rahmat Gul/AP Photo.

The U.S. Capitol is seen, as smoke from wildfires blankets the sky, Friday, July 17, 2026, in Washington. Rahmat Gul/AP Photo.

The Hub Staff
29 Jul 26

U.S. President Donald Trump has vowed to hit Canada with tariffs over wildfire smoke that has drifted south across the border into American cities. Critics say the threat reflects a petulant impulse to punish a neighbour for a natural disaster—one that is especially rich coming from a country whose own policies have imposed heavy costs on the global economy.

The threat adds wildfire smoke to the president’s list of grievances against Canada, further straining a trade relationship already battered by sweeping American duties. Fires burning across northern Ontario have blanketed much of eastern Canada in smoke before drifting over large swaths of the United States, prompting complaints from American commentators and elected officials about being subjected to a problem they say Canada has failed to manage. The vow lands amid an already volatile trade conflict, with the U.S. having imposed broad tariffs on most of its major trading partners since early 2025.

David Polansky, a Toronto-based writer, argued the tariff vow reflects a national reflex that has been on display throughout the crisis. Canadians, he observed, have met the fires with a certain measure of calm—citing the viral video of a CN freight train crew member coolly describing his situation to rescuers as his train was engulfed in flames—but also with a certain complacency about preventive measures such as thinning and controlled burning. “By contrast, the American response is typically to do something, and, barring that, to complain about the failure of others to also do something,” Polansky wrote.

That impulse to assign blame, he argued, sits awkwardly with Washington’s own record. “This is all a bit rich coming from citizens of a country that recently and without prior discussion (even amongst its own legislative body) decided to launch a still-unfinished war that has imposed grave costs on the global economy,” Polansky wrote, describing a petulance at work in the American reaction, including among elected representatives. Trump, he noted, has now added the smoke to his list of grievances in defence of raising tariffs on Canada.

The latest threat echoes long-standing warnings that tariffs under Trump function as ends in themselves rather than bargaining positions. Author and journalist David Frum argued in a 2024 conversation with The Hub‘s editor-at-large Sean Speer that the president’s protectionism is rooted in a zero-sum view of trade, making it unlikely that traditional concessions from Canada would ease the pressure. “Canadian policymakers must prepare for tariffs as an ideological end in themselves, rather than as leverage in a broader trade negotiation,” Frum said. Frum also urged Canadian policymakers to rethink their assumptions about engaging with the U.S., including closer alignment with Mexico to resist efforts to fragment North American trade.

Economic data from the first year of the tariff regime suggest the duties have not achieved their stated goals, according to Joseph Steinberg, a professor of economics at the University of Toronto. Steinberg noted the average U.S. tariff rate roughly tripled to approximately 13 percent, a level not seen since the early 1930s. Despite facing the lowest tariff rates among major American trading partners, Canadian exports to the U.S. fell by roughly 10 percent in real terms, he wrote—a decline that extended even to sectors facing no new duties, which he said pointed to a wider chill in the bilateral economic relationship. The annual U.S. goods trade deficit was virtually unchanged and American manufacturing employment continued to decline, Steinberg observed, evidence he argued that tariffs redistribute economic activity rather than expand it.

Within Canada, the smoke has fuelled its own search for culprits. Falice Chin, The Hub‘s Alberta bureau chief, reported that some domestic coverage has drawn a causal line between the wildfires and Alberta’s energy agenda, a framing that experts she interviewed rejected. “Few images capture public attention more powerfully than smoke turning the Toronto skyline orange. In moments like these, the search for a visible cause with familiar protagonists and antagonists can become almost irresistible,” Chin wrote.

The fires, meanwhile, continue to reshape life on the ground. The wildfire emergency has transformed Thunder Bay into a refuge for evacuees, while smoke from the surrounding boreal forest has at times drifted across central Canada and into the U.S., shrouding communities as far away as Toronto, Montreal, and New York.
