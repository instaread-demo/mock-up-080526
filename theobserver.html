Children's hearing screening clinics being offered at First Nations in Lambton County

Author of the article:
Paul Morden

Families living at First Nations in Lambton County will be able to access hearing screening clinics for children closer to home because of funding from Shell.

The company with a refinery and chemical plant near Corunna donated $100,000 during three years to help Pathways Health Centre for Children in Sarnia expand pediatric developmental and rehabilitation services at Aamjiwnaang, Kettle and Stony Point, and Walpole Island First Nations.

The donation grew out of an industry First Nation’s services focused partnership initiated by Pathways in 2017 and involving several companies located in what’s known locally as Chemical Valley.

“Shell has remained present over the last number of years, and there came another opportunity for us to revisit this conversation,” Pathways CEO Alison Morrison said.

That included asking each of the local First Nations about “what would make the biggest difference for you right now,” she said.

The centre provides therapy and other services to children and youth with physical, developmental and communications needs and last year went to more than 170 locations around Sarnia-Lambton to deliver services, as well as at its Sarnia site.

“And while we’re already in each of the First Nations providing services, not all of our services can go into communities,” Morrison said. “We realize that’s not always best or comfortable or accessible for families.”

“We want to deliver care where it is accessible and comfortable” and the donation will allow additional services to be taken to local First Nation communities, Morrison said.

“They all agree that audiology or hearing evaluation would be most beneficial at this time,” she said.

Currently, families have to travel into Sarnia for those services. “This helps us take it on the road,” Morrison said.

“If we can be working with partners in community, delivering the services in spaces, in the schools in the childcare (centres), in the community centres community members are already familiar with . . . that can be best for kids,” she said.

“Partnerships like this help move services into our communities where they are most accessible and most impactful,” Kimberly Bressette, chief of Kettle and Stony Point First Nation, said in a news release.

Hearing health is important for development, Morrison said.

“If there’s a child who is struggling with learning language or learning literacy, or interacting,” she said, “one of the questions we always ask is, what is their hearing like?”

“They are the next educators, health care professionals, tradespeople, leaders and parents, and it is so important that we provide them with the tools and support they need,” Shell vice-president Bonnie Yu said in the release.

Services provided by the Shell donation may look different in each community “and may evolve over the three years,” Morrison said.

“Year one, the focus is on hearing assessment screening,” she said.

Hearing clinics at the First Nations began in June.

Also new this year was a workshop Pathways held for 50 staff and leaders from summer camps around the community to help them serve an increasing number of campers who are neurodiverse, or in need of developmental, communication and behaviour support.

“This was an opportunity for our staff, who have some expertise, to share, maybe, some tips” camp staff can add “to their backpacks for summer camps” to help children “experience more success at camp,” Morrison said.

“We had a lot of great feedback, and this has the potential for significant impact,” she said. “We’re hoping to grow it next year.”
